== Language is the Material

=== The Column Name Problem

Fourteen million dollars is a lot of money to lose in a dictionary. The quarterly P&L reconciliation broke on a Tuesday. The numbers were perfect — every decimal in its place. It broke because the finance team’s chart of accounts classified a particular set of flows as "trading revenue," while the trading system that originated those same flows classified them as "market-making fees." Both were correct. Both had documentation. Both had been in production for years. And both produced a P&L that differed by fourteen million dollars depending on which system you asked.

Three hours of meeting followed. A finance director and a trading desk lead sat across from each other, each with a spreadsheet, each convinced the other’s numbers were wrong. Neither was wrong. They were speaking different languages. "Revenue" in the finance register included fee rebates netted against gross. "Revenue" in the trading register excluded them. The word was identical. The concept was not. Fourteen million dollars lived in the gap between two definitions of the same term.

Every automated check passed. Every pipeline ran green. The conflict was invisible to every tool in the stack because every tool operates on syntax, and syntactically the two uses of "revenue" were identical. Semantic. Linguistic. And until that Tuesday, completely undetected.

Most organizations cannot see the distinction between "missing data" and "missing vocabulary." They blame the pipeline. They almost never blame the names. The blindness is expensive: the fix for missing data is re-ingestion, which takes weeks; the fix for missing vocabulary is a definition update, which takes minutes.

AI is the forcing function, but the problem is architectural. Pipelines, transformations, SQL — implementation details. The material of the craft, the thing data engineers shape every day whether they realize it or not, is language.

=== Names Construct the Objects They Refer To

Most data engineers think of naming as a style decision. Pick a convention — snake_case, camelCase, with prefixes, without — enforce it, move on. This is the standard position, and it is wrong.

To name a column `revenue` is to make a commitment: this number, calculated this way, from these sources, with these exclusions, means THIS. Before the name, there was raw data — transactions, line items, adjustments, reversals. After the name, there is a governed concept that downstream consumers can rely on. Naming is construction.

If a definition is a contract — "when you use this term, you are bound to this meaning" — then breaking the contract breaks every downstream consumer. When the consumer is AI, the break skips the confused analyst who would have called you on Slack and goes straight to a confident, silent, wrong answer — presented to an executive or, worse, acted upon by an automated system.

At a major investment bank, the word "position" meant different things to the risk desk, the trading desk, and the back office. Three definitions, all internally consistent, all mutually incompatible. Humans navigated this by knowing which desk they were talking to. The data warehouse did not know which desk it was talking to. And so the reporting layer contained three different versions of "position," each correct within its own context, each dangerously wrong when used in someone else’s.

Industry treats this as a data quality problem. It is a language problem — specifically, polysemy: a single term carrying multiple meanings depending on context. Linguists have studied polysemy for a century. Data engineers rediscover it every time they build a warehouse.

A brokerage chart of accounts: three hundred and thirty-three GL accounts, which sounds reasonable until you realize they encode roughly forty business concepts crossed with six dimensions: asset class, related party status, client type, product, counterparty, and risk level. Instead of separating the concept from its dimensional context, the naming scheme bakes context into the identifier. Account number 41200-003-01 is a concept wearing a disguise made of dimensions. A human accountant can decode this because they have twenty years of tacit knowledge. An AI agent asked to "show me all revenue accounts" will miss half of them because the revenue concept is not explicit in the name but implicit in a dimensional encoding that nobody documented.

Naming-as-governance in miniature: three hundred and thirty-three entries when forty archetypes would suffice with proper vocabulary architecture. The naming scheme encodes too many dimensions into a single identifier, making it opaque to both humans and machines.

Table-role prefixes — `fact_`, `dim_`, `fct_` — are the same kind of mistake. (The convention is often credited to Kimball, but his books never prescribe it; the prefixes are a dbt-community style-guide convention.) They were governance decisions, correct at the time they were made. In row-based databases where joins were expensive, encoding the structural role of a table into its name was useful operational metadata. It told the query optimizer and the developer what to expect. But we do not live in that world anymore. In columnar warehouses (BigQuery, Snowflake, Databricks — the specific engine is immaterial), denormalized wide tables outperform star schemas. A semantic layer determines whether a field is a dimension or a measure at the field level, not the table level. The prefix is redundant — a governance decision that outlived the architecture it was governing.

Naming conventions are governance decisions, and governance decisions should be revisited when the architecture changes. Calling something `fact_orders` in a columnar database with a semantic layer is like requiring a card catalog in a library with a search engine. The card catalog was brilliant for its constraint. The constraint expired.

Namespaces are the deeper point. A naming convention styles identifiers; a namespace is the unit of governance. IAM policies, quality rules, and ownership are defined at the namespace level. A table named `trading.orders` inherits different governance than one named `staging_trading.raw_orders`. The name determines who can read the data, who can write to it, what quality checks apply, and who is accountable when something breaks. When you name a table, you are choosing a governance regime, not a label.

Naming decisions are among the most consequential decisions a data architect makes. Yet in most organizations, naming is delegated to individual developers with at most a style guide. Delegating contract law to the intern: the contracts still get written, they just do not protect anyone.

Some names go past unclear into actively misleading. A column called `volume` that contains notional value. A `balance` that means delta since last update, not current state. A `date` that refers to trade date in one system and settlement date in another. In each case, the name was plausible enough that nobody questioned it. The AI certainly did not question it — it used the column at face value and produced results that were internally consistent but factually wrong. Missing names produce errors you notice. Misleading names produce errors you trust.

=== The Metadata Graveyard

Every large organization has one. Ten thousand terms in a governance tool that nobody reads. A business glossary delivered to applause and abandoned within a year. The pattern is so consistent it qualifies as a law of organizational physics.

A senior leader decides the organization needs a business glossary. Consultants arrive. Five information architects, maybe more, each pulling down $150,000 to $250,000 a year. Workshops happen. Over six to twelve months, a comprehensive glossary is produced — hundreds or thousands of terms, each carefully defined, each vetted. Published in a standalone governance tool. Launch event. Applause. And then, slowly, silently, it dies.

It dies because the glossary was scoped as a deliverable, and a vocabulary is a living system. The funding ended the day the maintenance began. Data platforms change daily. New tables appear. Columns are renamed. Business concepts evolve. Models are deprecated and replaced. A vocabulary that is not updated with every change is a vocabulary that drifts from reality. And a vocabulary that drifts from reality is worse than no vocabulary at all, because it creates the illusion of governance where none exists.

Gartner estimates (February 2024) that 80 percent of data and analytics governance initiatives will fail by 2027 due to a lack of a real or manufactured crisis. As many as 90 percent fail at the first attempt — a Gartner estimate dating from the mid-2000s. Poor data quality costs organizations an average of $12.9 million per year, by Gartner's estimate. Users lose 30 to 40 percent of their time simply trying to find and understand information that already exists in their organization. The metadata graveyard is the default outcome, not a niche problem.

A senior data leader described traditional data catalogs as "expensive shelfware." Another: "Let’s visit a website just to browse the metadata, said no one ever." Nobody browses metadata recreationally. If the definitions are not in the path of the work — if you have to leave your IDE, open a browser, navigate to a catalog, and search for a term — you will not do it. And if you will not do it, the definitions might as well not exist.

FIBO — the Financial Industry Business Ontology, thousands of normative entities built in OWL by contributors from Goldman Sachs, Bloomberg, and Deutsche Bank, adopted almost nowhere — is the graveyard’s grandest monument. The full autopsy, from the OWL reasoners that had to be killed after days of processing to the practitioner verdict of "federation, not uniformity," is conducted in the Building with AI chapter.

Formalism is sound. Failure is in the distance between formalism and operational pipeline. An ontology that does not execute in the data pipeline does not govern the data. It describes the data from the outside, and descriptions from the outside drift. Any vocabulary system that exists separately from the systems it governs suffers this fate. The "boil the ocean" anti-pattern — one comprehensive ontology covering all business domains before shipping anything — is the most reliable way to spend two years and deliver nothing.

Standalone governance tools suffer the same critique with less severity. Collibra is governance-first: policy modeling, enterprise vocabularies, lifecycle management. Implementation timelines run three to nine months. User satisfaction among those who use the tool is high — around 87 percent. But satisfaction among users is a different metric than adoption across the organization. The governance team is twelve people. The organization has three thousand. The other 2,988 have never logged in.

Both vendors are pivoting toward AI — Collibra acquiring companies focused on unstructured data governance for LLMs, Alation acquiring firms specializing in AI agents over structured data. The irony is delicious: governance tools racing to embed AI at the same moment AI is making their original architecture obsolete.

The villain is neither the heavyweight ontology nor the standalone catalog but any architecture that puts governance outside the execution pipeline. Collibra is right that governance matters; wrong that it should live in a separate tool. FIBO is right that formal vocabulary matters; wrong that formal vocabulary requires OWL.

A deeper problem lurks beneath the catalog debate. The controlled-vocabulary tradition — formalized in ISO 11179 and instantiated in every modern data catalog product — was built for cross-reference, schema standardization, and demonstrable compliance. Real goals, well served. But the artefact is wrong, not just the maintenance model.

A short, structured definition tells you the datatype of a column and what the column is called. It does not tell you why the column exists, when its values diverge from the obvious interpretation, what edge cases bite practitioners every quarter, what the desk’s slang for it is, how it relates to upstream system quirks, or what a new analyst should know on their second day. All of this is information that exists in the heads of senior practitioners. It is precisely the information the controlled-vocabulary tradition has, throughout its history, failed to capture.

Tolerable when documentation consumers were humans who could absorb operational knowledge by sitting next to senior colleagues for six months. You learned the quirks of the P&L table by asking the person who built it. You learned that "the book" meant something specific in this office by overhearing it in meetings for three months. You learned which columns were vestigial by watching what the senior analysts used. None of this knowledge appeared in any catalog. It did not need to, because the humans who needed it had a channel — proximity, conversation, apprenticeship — through which to acquire it.

No such channel exists for an LLM agent. It has whatever you put in its context and nothing more. If the documentation says "revenue: total trading revenue in USD" and nothing else, that is the entirety of what the agent knows about revenue. It does not know that revenue excludes maker rebates in Q1 but includes them from Q2 onward due to a policy change. It does not know that the upstream system occasionally double-counts fee reversals during month-end settlement windows. It does not know that the desk calls the field "top-line" and never uses the word "revenue" in conversation. Formally correct. Operationally useless. The agent will produce answers consistent with the definition and inconsistent with reality.

Metadata graveyards were always failing to close this gap, even for human consumers. AI merely makes the failure impossible to ignore.

=== Documentation as Onboarding

ISO 11179 tells you a column’s type. A senior colleague tells you why the column lies on the last day of the month. Only one of these prevents a production incident.

When a new analyst joins a trading desk, nobody hands them a metadata catalog. They get prose, and they get conversation. Someone tells them the war stories: the time the upstream system reclassified counterparties without warning and broke the morning P&L for a week. Someone explains the conventions: how P&L is computed here, which of the three canonical definitions the desk uses, when it switches between them. Someone walks them through the slang: what "the book" means in this office, which fields get used and which are vestigial. The onboarding is narrative, contextual, and specific to how this particular team uses these particular data assets.

A capable LLM brings substantial general knowledge to any business domain. Accounting principles, market mechanics, regulatory shape, the standard patterns of analytical work in finance — on day one, that general knowledge frequently exceeds what a junior human analyst arrives with. A fresh graduate joining a treasury desk does not typically know the mechanics of tri-party repo or the regulatory treatment of rehypothecated collateral. The LLM does. What it lacks is what the human will spend the next month acquiring: organizational nuance, desk-specific convention, tribal slang, the operational craft of how this particular team uses these particular data assets.

After about a month, the human analyst has absorbed enough context to function. They know which of the three revenue definitions the desk uses. They know that "the cross" means the cross-currency basis swap book, not the FX cross rate. They know that the 4pm snapshot is the one that matters and the 3:30pm snapshot is vestigial but still produced because a downstream system depends on it. Without equivalent context delivered explicitly, the agent plateaus at general competence — correct in the abstract, wrong in the particular. The particular is where the work happens.

Documentation that closes this gap looks like onboarding material, not ISO 11179.

What works is the domain essay: six hundred to fifteen hundred words of considered prose covering provenance, conventions, edge cases, slang, history, and craft for a single dataset or a tightly coupled cluster of them. Written by the people who know the data — not generated from the schema, which by construction cannot tell you what the schema fails to capture. The essay is anchored on structured metadata that does not disappear: datatypes, primary keys, refresh cadence, lineage references, ownership records. These anchors are the index into the documentation, not the documentation itself.

Structured metadata — the ISO 11179 anchors of datatype, key, classification — remains essential. Machine-readable, automatable, suitable for cross-reference and integration. But the semantic weight has moved: in an agentic architecture, the essay is the primary carrier of meaning, and structured metadata is its index.

The domain essay is the operational note a senior practitioner writes for a new colleague: dense, deliberate, evidence-based, free of padding. Prose, because the consumer changed; the standard did not.

Essay-based documentation works because it follows the discipline of code. Versioned. Dated revisions. Named authors and reviewers. Stored alongside the data assets it describes. And a decay model: an essay reviewed within the last twelve months is treated differently from one untouched for three years. Stale documentation is, for an agent, strictly worse than absent documentation — the agent reads stale prose with the same confidence as fresh prose and produces confidently wrong answers. A documentation system that does not manage decay produces worse outcomes the longer it operates.

Documentation that lets an agent reason over data is documentation that onboards a new human analyst fastest. The agent, the new analyst, and the auditor converge on the same artefact. Build documentation for agents and it gets better for humans. Build it for cataloging and it has never been good enough for either.

=== The Three-Tier Vocabulary

Three tiers, connected by a single principle: upstream always wins.


[#fig-three-tier-vocab]
.The three-tier vocabulary: glossary governs schema governs semantic layer. Upstream always wins.
image::figures/three-tier-vocab.png[three tier vocab,width=600,align="center"]

Tier 1 is the glossary. This is the canonical source of business meaning. It contains the organization’s controlled vocabulary: defined terms, with relationships between them — hierarchies, synonyms, associations. The glossary is business-owned, not technology-owned. It does not contain SQL. It does not reference specific tables or columns. It speaks in business language about business concepts. "Trading volume" is defined here. "Revenue" is defined here. "Position" is defined here, with enough precision that two reasonable people reading the definition would agree on what it means.

Terms are nodes in a semantic graph. "Revenue" does not exist in isolation. It exists in relation to "volume," "position," "trade," "fee," "rebate." Meaning is partly constituted by relationships between terms. A glossary that treats terms as isolated definitions — a flat list with descriptions — is missing the point. A vocabulary that treats terms as a network, with explicit relationships, captures how meaning works in a business domain. An AI agent that knows "revenue" is a type of "financial metric" that is calculated from "trades" and excludes "rebates" can reason about revenue in ways that an agent with just a string definition cannot.

Tools constrain what ontologies can express. A YAML file in a code repository yields flat, static, developer-facing text. A governed system with relational semantics yields hierarchies, synonyms, associations, and machine-queryable relationships. Something richer than a flat file, well short of OWL.

Tier 2 is the schema definition file — the model metadata that lives alongside the transformation code (dbt’s `schema.yml` in the current implementation, though the principle applies to any SQL-based modelling framework). The schema definition IS the logical data model. No second artifact stands behind it.

In traditional data architecture, the logical data model is a separate artifact — an ER diagram, a data dictionary, a design document — created during design and then implemented in code. Two artifacts, one truth, inevitable divergence. Every data architect has lived this: a beautiful ER diagram on Confluence that was accurate eighteen months ago. The actual tables look nothing like it. Nobody updates the diagram because nobody looks at it.

When model documentation and model implementation are the same file, they cannot diverge. You cannot change the model without changing the documentation, because they are the same artifact. The schema constitutes the data’s meaning rather than describing it from outside. A schema change is a material transformation of what the data can mean, however blandly the ticket says "metadata update."

"But we need design review before implementation." Yes — and in a CI/CD pipeline with code review, the schema definition IS reviewed before deployment. There is no useful separation between "design" and "implementation" when the design artifact is executable. A separate LDM document is a copy wearing design’s clothes. Copies drift. This one will too.

Tier 2 inherits from Tier 1. If the glossary says "trading volume" means the total number of units traded in a given period, then the schema description of the `trading_volume` column says the same thing — not a paraphrase, not a reinterpretation, but the same definition. Upstream always wins. If a Tier 2 description contradicts a Tier 1 definition, the Tier 1 definition is correct and the Tier 2 description must be updated. The rule is enforced — ideally by automation, practically by code review, minimally by convention.

Tier 3 is the semantic layer — the consumption-layer configuration that maps business concepts to data (LookML, dbt Semantic Layer, Cube, or equivalent). This is where definitions become queryable. The semantic layer does not create new definitions. It inherits them from Tier 2, which inherited them from Tier 1. What the semantic layer adds is navigation: how concepts relate to each other for the purpose of answering questions, what the default aggregations are, what filters are commonly applied, what hierarchies structure the data for exploration.

When an AI agent needs to answer a question about revenue, it consults the semantic layer, which tells it which table contains revenue data, how revenue is calculated, what dimensions are available, what caveats apply. Without a semantic layer, the agent guesses — picking the wrong column, inventing a calculation, joining tables incorrectly. With one, it grounds its reasoning in governed definitions. The difference between guessing and grounding is whether you built the vocabulary.

When AI is the consumer, naming decisions have cost implications that make CFOs pay attention. A star schema requires the AI to reason about joins — which tables to connect, through which keys, in what order. Every join is additional tokens consumed, additional reasoning steps, additional opportunities for error. A flat, denormalized Gold table with a semantic layer requires no join reasoning. The AI reads the semantic layer, identifies the right table, and queries it directly. In practice, this reduces token consumption by a factor of three to five. Your naming conventions and architectural choices are cost decisions, not just semantic ones. Bad names are expensive.

A quality standard applies across all three tiers. ISO 11179 — the international standard for metadata registries — provides criteria that every definition should meet: precise, unambiguous, non-circular, stated in natural language, domain-scoped. The standard has been actively maintained since the 1990s and was updated most recently in 2023 and 2024. It is the foundational reference for metadata in government, healthcare, and regulated industries. Australia, Canada, Germany, the US, and the UK all maintain ISO 11179-aligned registries. A Texas state agency published a standard grounded in 11179 that begins with a statement so obvious it should not need saying: "To be data-driven, an organization must be metadata-driven."

The standard is excellent; the failure is in how organizations hold it — as a compliance exercise rather than a quality tool. The three-tier vocabulary uses 11179 criteria the way a newsroom uses a style guide: as craft discipline. Every definition should pass 11179 scrutiny. But the definitions live in the pipeline, not in a separate registry. The standard governs the quality. The architecture governs the location.

Domain essays carry the semantic weight; structured metadata indexes them. Tier 1 names the concepts, Tier 2 binds them to columns, Tier 3 exposes them to consumers. But the explanation of why a column exists, the documentation of edge cases that bite every quarter, the operational craft that separates a competent analyst from a textbook one — that lives in the essay alongside and above the tiers.

"Upstream always wins" extends to documentation. The glossary defines the term. The schema definition binds it. The semantic layer exposes it. But the essay — written by domain experts, versioned like code, decaying visibly when stale — is what the agent reads when it needs to reason, not retrieve. Retrieval asks "what is this column called and what is its datatype?" Reasoning asks "why does this column behave unexpectedly during month-end, and what should a competent analyst do about it?" Only the essay answers the second question. The tiers are the skeleton. The essay is the knowledge.

This architecture works because it solves the maintenance problem that kills every other approach. The glossary changes slowly — business concepts evolve over quarters and years, not days. The schema definition changes at the pace of development — when models are added, modified, or deprecated. The semantic layer changes at the pace of consumption — when new questions arise or new navigational paths are needed. Each tier has its own cadence, its own owners, and its own change management process. But they are connected by a single flow: upstream always wins. Governance becomes an architectural property. The system governs itself through its own structure.

The standalone governance tool cannot replicate this. Collibra, or any similar platform, sits outside the execution pipeline. Definitions in Collibra do not automatically propagate to the schema definition or the semantic layer. When a developer changes a column name in the transformation tool, they do not automatically update Collibra. Two systems, two truths, inevitable drift. The three-tier vocabulary eliminates this drift not through discipline but through architecture. The tiers are connected. Changes propagate. Upstream always wins. Governance by construction.

Governance outside the execution pipeline is aspiration, not governance. It exists as documentation. It exists as a quarterly meeting where people review proposed changes to the data dictionary and approve or reject them. By the time a change is approved through a committee process, the data has already changed three more times. The committee is governing a system that no longer exists. Governance by committee is governance by nostalgia.

*Pattern: Three-Tier Vocabulary*

_Context:_ An organization needs governed definitions that flow consistently from business language to data infrastructure to consumption interfaces.

_Problem:_ Without a tiered architecture, definitions are maintained independently at each level — the glossary says one thing, the schema describes another, and the semantic layer presents a third. Contradictions accumulate silently.

_Solution:_ Three tiers, with upstream always winning. Tier 1 (Business Glossary): canonical definitions owned by business stakeholders, stored in a machine-readable format. Tier 2 (Schema Definition): transformation tool schema files inherit Tier 1 definitions verbatim — the schema description IS the glossary definition, not a paraphrase. Tier 3 (Semantic Model): the consumption-layer definitions inherit from Tier 2. No tier may introduce its own definition. No downstream system may override an upstream meaning. Changes propagate top-down: change the glossary, the schema description updates, the semantic model updates.

_Example:_ Glossary defines "trading_volume" as "total quantity of assets exchanged in a given period, excluding test accounts and internal transfers." The schema definition file for `fct_executions` inherits this definition verbatim on the `trading_volume` measure. The semantic model’s `trading_volume` measure inherits from the schema. An analyst querying through any interface sees the same definition. A change to the definition happens once, in Tier 1, and propagates automatically.

_Consequences:_ Upstream-always-wins is rigid. Domains cannot experiment with local terminology without a glossary change. Fast-moving teams feel constrained.

=== Why This Is Linguistics

"Data engineering is applied linguistics" is a literal claim. The problems data engineers encounter every day are problems that linguists named and studied decades ago.

Synonymy is the most visible: multiple terms for the same concept. Every controlled vocabulary is an attempt to eliminate synonyms within a specific domain. When a data platform has three names for the same metric, it has a synonymy problem. "Pick one and rename the others" is the reflex and the mistake. A controlled vocabulary maps all three names to a single canonical term, preserving the synonyms as aliases for discoverability while enforcing the canonical term for governance.

"Position" at the investment bank is polysemy: one term with multiple meanings depending on context. Polysemy is a feature of natural language that makes communication efficient in context and disastrous out of context. A data platform is, by definition, out of context — it aggregates data from multiple contexts into a single layer. If your vocabulary does not disambiguate polysemous terms, your platform inherits every ambiguity from every source system.

GL taxonomy inflation is a failure of dimensional separation: encoding multiple independent dimensions into a single identifier. Linguists call this compounding — think of German compound nouns that pack multiple concepts into a single word. In a chart of accounts, compounding asset class, client type, and risk level into a single account number creates the same problem: the concepts are present but not individually addressable. You cannot ask "show me all revenue accounts" because "revenue" is not a separate axis in the naming scheme; it is fused into an opaque compound.

AI returning zeros for collateral values because the table was not labeled properly — a referential gap. The data existed. The concept existed. The mapping between them was missing. Organizations blame the data when the problem is the name.

Data engineers face linguistic problems and need linguistic tools: controlled vocabularies, taxonomies, thesauri, semantic networks. Twenty years of investment in pipeline tools — orchestration, transformation, testing — and comparatively nothing in vocabulary tools. The imbalance reflects a conceptual misunderstanding about what the job is.

Open the job posting for a "Senior Data Engineer" at any major company. SQL, Python, Spark, Airflow, dbt, Terraform, Kubernetes. Maybe a cloud certification. Nowhere: "ability to construct and maintain a controlled vocabulary." Nowhere: "experience with semantic modeling." Nowhere: "skill in extracting precise definitions from domain experts." Yet those are the skills that determine whether the data platform produces clarity or confusion. The industry hires for pipeline competence and wonders why the vocabulary is a mess.

Industry is starting to notice. Common vocabulary as a foundational principle of modern data architecture is accelerating as a recognized prerequisite, but the recognition is still mostly rhetorical. Investment — in tools, in people, in organizational attention — has not caught up.

Without formal vocabulary infrastructure, business meaning scatters across schema descriptions, semantic layer definitions, tribal knowledge, and Slack threads. Everybody talking, nobody speaking the same language.

At one organization, the revenue mapping rules lived in a CSV file that nobody owned. Seven trading symbols were missing from the CSV, which meant trades in those assets were generating no journal entries. Nobody had noticed because the engineers who wrote the code did not understand the domain, and the domain experts had left. Code owned execution. Nobody owned semantics. Revenue was not a governed concept. It was a prayer.

Vocabulary architecture prevents this. When "revenue" has a definition that is canonical, machine-readable, and embedded in the execution pipeline, code and meaning cannot separate. Read the model, read the definition. Change the model, change the definition. Same artifact.

When vocabulary is not formalized, every question routes through a person. "What does this column mean?" Ask Sam. "How is this calculated?" Ask Keith. "Why does this number not match the other number?" Ask whoever built the original dashboard, if they still work here. Knowledge lives in people’s heads, and every query becomes a dependency on a specific individual.

One team lead: "My team literally don’t know the domain. Sam and Keith are the only guys that know. And that’s a problem." It is a problem — and a predictable consequence of an architecture that does not capture vocabulary. When vocabulary exists only in people’s heads, personnel changes are data losses.

Most organizations hit a phase transition — scaling from one office to multiple, or private to public — where tacit knowledge stops working. When everyone sat in the same room, tacit knowledge worked — proximity created shared context. When teams go global, tacit knowledge breaks. The meaning has to be written down, and if it is written down in a PDF on SharePoint, it will be lost within six months. It has to be written down in the pipeline.

The U.S. Department of Defense — twenty-nine languages, twenty-four countries — published a controlled vocabulary framework that makes the point with military directness: "When terms are not immediately and unerringly understood by every enterprise participant, there is a significant risk that process outcomes and analytic results will be inconsistent." Replace "enterprise participant" with "AI agent" and you have the thesis of this essay. If the DoD understands that vocabulary is infrastructure, data platforms have no excuse.

Organizations that navigate this transition successfully recognize what they are building: a shared, governed, machine-readable language for talking about their business. The platform is infrastructure. The language is the product.

=== Domain Registers and Code-Switching

When a trader and an accountant both say "position," they are having two different conversations with the same word. The data warehouse, unable to tell which conversation it is in, joins both and calls the result a report.


[#fig-polysemy-position]
.Polysemy: “position” means three different things to three domains. Without a semantic layer the warehouse joins them into nonsense.
image::figures/polysemy-position.png[polysemy position,width=600,align="center"]

Sociolinguists use the term "register" to describe a systematic variation in language that correlates with context of use: vocabulary, grammar, and assumptions shift depending on who is speaking, to whom, and about what. A doctor discussing a patient with a colleague uses a different register than the same doctor explaining the same condition to the patient. Same information, different encoding. Competence lies in knowing which register to deploy and when.

Every business domain operates in its own register. Trading speaks in a register of immediacy and exposure: positions are open or closed, risk is directional, time is measured in milliseconds or at most trading sessions. Finance speaks in a register of periodicity and accountability: the same underlying activity becomes a journal entry, a line in a ledger, a component of a P&L that must reconcile to the cent at month-end. Treasury speaks in a register of liquidity and obligation: what matters is not what was traded but what is owed, what is available, what can be pledged. Custody speaks in a register of safekeeping and proof: the asset is held or it is not, and the chain of custody must be unbroken and auditable.

The registers do more than relabel a shared reality; they construct different realities. When a trader says "position," the word denotes a live directional exposure — something with a mark-to-market value right now, changing with every tick. When a finance professional says "position," the word denotes a net accounting exposure — aggregated across trades, netted by instrument and counterparty, valued at a point in time for reporting. The trader’s position is kinetic. The accountant’s position is archival. Same word. Meaning constituted by register.

Linguistics calls this code-switching: the systematic shift between registers that competent speakers navigate unconsciously but that machines need explicit mappings to handle. A senior analyst who has worked across trading and finance for a decade can code-switch mid-sentence without effort — "the position generated a journal entry that affected the P&L position" — and every human in the room understands that the first "position" is trading-register and the second is finance-register. The shift is unmarked; context disambiguates. A machine cannot perform this disambiguation unless the vocabulary infrastructure explicitly models domain scoping. Without it, the machine treats both occurrences as the same concept, because they are the same string. From that false identity: wrong joins, wrong aggregations, wrong numbers presented with full confidence.

Cross-domain polysemy is a structural feature of the three-tier vocabulary, designed for rather than patched around. Tier 1, the glossary, defines domain-scoped entries. `finance.position` and `trading.position` are different concepts that share a lexeme. No single definition broad enough to cover both. Definitional broadening — "position: a quantity of an asset held or owed by an entity" — is the hallmark of a vocabulary that has given up on precision. It covers everything and means nothing. Instead, the glossary maintains separate entries, each precise within its domain, with an explicit relationship between them: these terms share a lexeme but differ in domain scope, calculation method, temporal granularity, and governance owner.

Tier 2 implements each domain-scoped concept as a model in its respective namespace. The trading domain has `trading.positions` with fields that reflect the trading register: instrument, direction, entry price, current mark, unrealized P&L. The finance domain has `finance.positions` with fields that reflect the finance register: instrument, net quantity, book value, fair value, accounting standard applied. The schema definitions make explicit what human speakers leave implicit. They are, in effect, the grammar of each register written out in full.

Tier 3, the semantic layer, provides the navigation that resolves which "position" the user means based on conversational context. When a user asks "what is our EUR rates position?" the answer depends entirely on register. If the question follows a series of queries about trading volume and order flow, the semantic layer should resolve to the trading register. If the question follows queries about monthly P&L and revenue recognition, it should resolve to the finance register. The semantic layer uses the accumulated context of the conversation — domain of prior queries, metrics already referenced, dimensions already filtered — to determine which register is active. Machine code-switching: the operational expression of the linguistic theory.

Cross-register problems are harder than within-register problems. Within the trading domain, vocabulary is relatively stable: traders agree on what "position" means because they share a register. Within finance, the same is true. The conflicts erupt at the boundaries, when a query begins in one register and must compose with another. A question like "what was the P&L impact of our largest trading positions last quarter?" requires the system to identify positions in the trading register (largest by notional exposure), then compose with the finance register (P&L impact as calculated by accounting rules), resolving the temporal mismatch (trading positions are real-time, P&L is periodic) and the aggregation mismatch (trading positions are per-instrument, P&L may be per-book or per-entity). This is register composition, and it is where most data platforms fail silently.

Silent failure, because each domain’s numbers are individually correct. The error emerges only in the composition — in the join, in the mapping, in the assumption that "position" in one system corresponds to "position" in another. A human analyst with cross-domain experience catches this because they can code-switch, because they know that the trading system’s position and the finance system’s position are related but not identical. An analyst without that experience, or an AI agent without explicit register mappings, will join on the shared lexeme and produce a number that looks right and is not.

Domain registers are an architectural requirement. Each domain is a register. Each register has its own controlled vocabulary. Cross-register mappings are explicit, governed artifacts — not implicit assumptions buried in join logic. When a Gold table combines data from trading and finance, the schema definition must document which register each field belongs to and how the cross-register composition was resolved. Documentation here is the semantic contract that makes the composition trustworthy.

At a trading venue, register boundaries are particularly sharp. A single trade generates events that propagate through four registers almost simultaneously. In the trading register, it is an execution: instrument, price, quantity, timestamp, counterparty. In the custody register, it is a settlement obligation: assets must move between custody accounts, with an unbroken settlement record (CSD confirmation). In the finance register, it is a revenue event: the fee is recognized, the journal entry is created, the P&L impact is calculated. In the treasury register, it is a liquidity event: the venue’s available reserves have changed, and the treasury position must be updated. One trade, four registers, four vocabularies, four sets of assumptions about what matters and what does not. A data platform serving all four domains must be explicitly multilingual — four registers, four sets of domain-scoped definitions, and a composition layer that maps between them.

A single flat vocabulary with one definition of each term is the approach most organizations attempt and most regret. It produces definitions so broad they are useless, or definitions so narrow they apply to only one domain while misleading the others. Register-based vocabulary is harder to build — more definitions, more explicit mappings, more governance. But it produces a vocabulary that reflects how the business operates: a federation of domains, each with its own register, connected through explicit and governed translations.

No natural language has a single register. English does not speak the same way in a courtroom, a laboratory, a trading floor, and a kitchen. A competent data platform must navigate the same shifts — not through a large language model that approximates register-awareness from training data, but through explicit vocabulary infrastructure that models registers as first-class architectural concepts.

*Pattern: Domain Register*

_Context:_ Multiple business domains use overlapping vocabulary — the same word means different things in different domains.

_Problem:_ Cross-domain queries silently conflate domain-specific meanings. "Position" in Trading (current holdings) is not "position" in Treasury (cash and liquidity allocation). An agent or analyst using the wrong definition produces wrong answers without warning.

_Solution:_ Each domain operates in a linguistic register — a self-consistent vocabulary with its own conventions, slang, and default assumptions. The register is documented explicitly: which terms are domain-specific, what they mean in this domain, and how they differ from the same term in other domains. When a query crosses domains, register switching is explicit — the system loads the target domain’s register and resolves any polysemous terms through the bridge morphism.

_Example:_ Trading register: "position" = current holdings by instrument. Treasury register: "position" = cash and liquidity allocation by currency. When an agent encounters "position" in a cross-domain query, the register switching protocol identifies which domain’s definition applies. If both are needed, the bridge morphism documents how Trading.position and Treasury.position relate (and where they do not).

_Consequences:_ Maintaining explicit register documentation for every domain is ongoing work. Registers drift if not reviewed.

*Pattern: Polysemy Resolution*

_Context:_ A term appears in multiple domain registers with different meanings.

_Problem:_ Without explicit resolution, the system picks one meaning silently — typically whichever table appears first in the schema or whichever domain the agent was initialized in. The consumer does not know which meaning was used.

_Solution:_ Polysemous terms are flagged in the vocabulary with explicit cross-domain mappings. Each domain’s definition stands within its register. The bridge morphism between domains documents how the definitions relate: whether they are the same concept viewed differently (Trading.Instrument ≈ Finance.FinancialProduct), overlapping but non-identical (Trading.volume ≠ Finance.revenue_volume), or unrelated homonyms (Trading.book ≠ Accounting.book). When an agent or semantic layer encounters a polysemous term in a cross-domain context, the resolution is governed and declared, not inferred.

_Example:_ "Revenue" in Finance = net recognized revenue per GAAP. "Revenue" in Trading = gross execution value. An agent asked "What is our revenue?" in a cross-domain context loads both definitions, identifies the polysemy, and either asks the consumer which definition they mean or defaults to the requesting domain’s register with an explicit declaration: "Using Finance definition of revenue (net recognized, GAAP). Trading uses a different definition (gross execution value)."

_Consequences:_ Cross-domain queries become slower (resolution overhead) and more complex to debug when polysemy maps conflict.

=== Why Data Engineering Is Applied Linguistics

Type systems and semantic layers are structurally isomorphic, and making this explicit reframes what data architecture is. A type system — the kind studied in programming language theory and mathematical logic — tells you what operations are valid on what objects. You cannot add a string to a number, not because the computer cannot do it, but because the type system says it is meaningless. A semantic layer does the same thing for data: it tells you what queries are valid on what tables, what aggregations make sense for what measures, what hierarchies structure what dimensions. A type system and a semantic layer are the same discipline applied at different levels of abstraction. (This is not incidental to the author’s perspective — a background in type theory tends to make this structural parallel impossible to unsee.)

Data architecture is, at its core, the management of ontological commitments. When you define a Gold table, you are committing to a specific way of carving up the world. "These are our customers. These are our orders. These are our positions." Nature hands you none of those categories; your modeling decisions construct them. A different organization, looking at the same raw data, would construct different categories. A different modeler within the same organization would construct slightly different categories — not wrong, just choices. But choices that, once made, constrain everything downstream. Every query, every dashboard, every AI agent reasons within the constraints of your ontological commitments. If the commitments are wrong, everything downstream is wrong — green pipelines, broken vocabulary.

People who understand that meaning is constructed, that definitions are contractual, that the material of the work is language itself — linguists, librarians, logicians, or engineers who think like them — should be shaping these commitments. Pipelines matter. SQL matters. Orchestration matters. The hardest part of data architecture is the vocabulary: deciding what "revenue" means, where the boundary between "trading" and "market-making" falls, whether "position" is one concept or three. These are linguistic decisions with engineering consequences, and the industry has systematically underinvested in the people and tools needed to make them well.

"This sounds great in theory, but I have two thousand tables and no glossary. Where do I even start?" Eighteen months ago that objection won. The economics of curation have shifted: a comprehensive vocabulary that once took a dedicated team years to build is now within reach of one person working with AI. The maintenance problem that killed every previous attempt at vocabulary architecture is solvable when AI handles the maintenance.

The bottleneck has shifted from labor to quality of thinking. You still need someone who understands both the domain and the technology. You still need someone who can sit with a finance stakeholder for an hour and extract the difference between "gross revenue" and "net revenue" and "recognized revenue" and "booked revenue." You still need someone who can say: these are four different concepts, and here is why, and here is what each one means, and here is how they relate to each other. That is human work — and the most valuable work on the data platform.

Most data platforms are filing cabinets without an index. Thousands of tables, millions of rows, terabytes of storage — and without a vocabulary that organizes, defines, and relates the concepts those tables contain, the platform stores data the way a warehouse stores boxes: faithfully, efficiently, and without any understanding of what is inside them. Vocabulary transforms storage into knowledge.

Stack-agnostic. Examples throughout name specific tools — dbt, LookML, BigQuery — because they are drawn from practice. The three-tier vocabulary works equally well with any transformation framework, any semantic layer tool, any columnar warehouse, or any agent orchestration tool. The tools are interchangeable. The vocabulary architecture is not.

For organizations operating across borders, vocabulary architecture must account for multilingual contexts. In a global organization, business concepts may be discussed in English in one office, Mandarin in another, German in a third. The three-tier structure helps here: the glossary can hold multilingual definitions — the same concept expressed in multiple languages — while the schema definitions and the semantic layer operate in English as a lingua franca. The canonical term is language-independent; its expression is not. Solvable within the architecture, but a problem most vocabulary initiatives ignore until it bites.

=== Vocabulary Before Pipelines

If you are running a data platform and you think your job is about pipelines and transformations and SQL, you are describing the plumbing and ignoring the water supply. The material of the work — the thing that determines whether the platform creates value or confusion — is language. The words chosen for columns, tables, domains, definitions. Those words are the interface between an organization’s knowledge and every consumer of its data, human or machine.

Always true. What changed is that AI makes it undeniable. A human encountering a badly named column can ask someone, check source code, apply tribal knowledge. An AI encountering the same column either guesses or fails. No workaround. No tribal knowledge. Only what the name says and what the definition provides. If the name is misleading and the definition is missing, the AI is worse than useless — it is confidently wrong.

Gartner estimates that through 2026, organizations will abandon 60 percent of AI projects unsupported by AI-ready data. Sixty-three percent of organizations either lack or are unsure they have the right data management practices for AI. Forty percent of business initiatives fail due to poor data quality. Default outcomes of building AI on ungoverned vocabulary. The technology works. The data exists. The vocabulary does not.

First: invest in vocabulary before anything else. Before the new dashboard, before the AI agent, before the transformation refactor — define terms. Start with the twenty most important business concepts. Get stakeholders in a room and define them in the schema definition that IS the model — no PDF, no governance tool outside the pipeline. Machine-readable, precise, canonical. Days, not months. Saves years.

Second: kill the metadata graveyard. A business glossary that has not been updated in six months is a liability, not a glossary. Either embed it in your execution pipeline — connect it to your schema definitions so that changes propagate automatically — or delete it. A stale glossary creates the illusion of governance, and the illusion is more dangerous than the absence.

Third: upstream always wins. If the glossary says "trading volume" means X, then every downstream system says "trading volume" means X — the same definition, not a paraphrase. A downstream system that wants a different concept gets a different name. "Trading volume" is taken. Rigid at the definitional level to enable flexibility at every other level.

Fourth: naming conventions are governance decisions. Review them with the same rigor applied to IAM policies — they determine the same thing: who can do what with which data, and what the data means when they do it.

Fifth: the scarce resource on any data platform is vocabulary — the quality of definitions, the precision of naming, the rigor of semantic relationships — not engineers, compute, or storage. Most organizations have more people working on their transformation layer than on their vocabulary. Reverse that ratio, or at least balance it.

Technology changes enormously across years and industries. Problems do not. "What does this column mean?" "Why does this number not match that number?" "Who defined this term and when did the definition change?" Always vocabulary. Always linguistic. Regardless of stack.

Your AI agent will be exactly as intelligent as your vocabulary allows it to be. Model quality is upstream of you. Training data is upstream of you. Infrastructure is upstream of you. The one thing entirely within your control is whether you built the vocabulary — the unglamorous, difficult, deeply human work of defining terms.

Every organization that deploys an AI agent without a governed vocabulary has built a confident new employee, handed it a desk and a database login, and forgotten to tell it what any of the words mean.
