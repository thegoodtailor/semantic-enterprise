== Agentic Intelligence

=== Beyond the Answer

Conversational analytics, done properly, is Stage 1: a human asks a question in plain language, an agent answers it through the governed semantic layer, and the answer can be trusted. If you have built this, you are ahead of most of the market. You are also at the beginning, because the loop is reactive. A human asks. The system answers. The human drives.

Most organizations think Stage 1 is the destination. They ship a chatbot, connect it to their warehouse, run a demo for the executive team, and declare victory. The demo always works; the questions are canned or at least friendly. Then the thing goes into production and the hard questions arrive — questions nobody pre-tested, questions that cross domains, questions where the answer depends on when you ask — and the system quietly fails. It returns a number that is almost right, or right for last month but not this month, and nobody notices, because nobody has a baseline to check against.

Stage 2 is agentic intelligence: the estate watched by agents that raise what matters without being asked. An agent that watches trading volumes overnight and flags an anomaly before the London desk opens. An agent that notices a pattern in settlement failures across counterparties and generates a hypothesis about why. Each of those flags is, in the terms of "The Death of the Dashboard", a standing question's newest published answer — identity, diff against yesterday, subscriber list. Machinery, not charm.

Stage 3 is autonomous research: agents that maintain and grow knowledge bases, test hypotheses against live data over weeks rather than turns, and retire their own stale conclusions. A junior research analyst — quantitative, tireless, with complete recall of the firm's recorded history — whose work still terminates in a human's judgment.

Both stages run on one piece of machinery, and this chapter builds it: a loop. The agent *observes* the estate through standing questions. It *conjectures* — writes beliefs into governed Silver. Its conjectures are *tested*, against the data and against other agents built to refute them. What survives is *published* to the answer ledger, graded and attested. And then the step that changes what your platform is: a conclusion that survives becomes a *proposal* — a new standing question, a new measure, a new Gold contract, a new pipeline — drafted by the agent itself, in the creole, entering the estate through the population contracts of "By Any Means Necessary" and the build gates of "Building with AI". New knowledge becomes new schema. The estate grows itself.

The industry's agent cancellations list the same three causes every time: escalating costs, unclear business value, inadequate risk controls. Read them against the loop and each names a missing piece of it. Escalating costs are monitoring without standing questions and a ledger — every question re-derived on every run, no cadence, no cache, no unit price per insight. Unclear business value is what agents produce when their conclusions have nowhere governed to land and nothing compounds. Inadequate risk controls are agentic writes without attestation, trust grades, or refutation. The gap between ambition and execution is an infrastructure gap, not an intelligence gap — and the infrastructure it names is this loop's.

The strongest public evidence for autonomous research seems, at first read, to argue against everything below it in the stack: the celebrated Stage 3 systems ran with no governed warehouse and no semantic layer, and this chapter's closing section names them. They ran in domains where verification is free — code executes or it does not. Your enterprise gets no free verdicts. That difference is the argument of the chapter's close: the stack is how an enterprise builds the oracle that software engineering was given for free.

=== What an Agent Is

"Agent" has been abused to the point of meaninglessness. A form-filling chatbot is now an "agent." A scheduled script that sends Slack alerts is an "agent." A RAG pipeline that looks up documents before answering is an "agent." Useful things, all of them. Not agents.

An agent, in the sense that matters architecturally, has what a chatbot lacks: memory, goals, and agency. Memory is persistent knowledge of what it has previously concluded, what data it has seen, and what hypotheses it is tracking. Goals reach past "answer this question" to "monitor this domain for anomalies" and "test whether this hypothesis still holds." Agency is the capacity to originate work: to decide what to examine next, which conjecture to test, when to publish, and when to stay silent.

Agents also act beyond the estate. They trigger workflows, call services, drive infrastructure; some agents order parts and some switch the lights off. This book governs none of that, and pretends to govern none of it. Its subject is the estate's surface of agency: what an agent reads from the estate arrives assembled and checked, and what an agent writes into it lands attested, graded, and evidenced under the population laws. A system that acts on an agent's conclusion — moving money, paging a human, rebalancing a book — is acting on a published answer with its trust grade and provenance attached, and that is the estate's contribution to every risk conversation downstream of it.

Goals demand more than definitions. The agent needs domain heuristics — the tacit knowledge an experienced analyst carries. In trading, a sudden spike in volume at three in the morning is not the same signal as a spike at market open. In treasury, a liquidity position that looks healthy in aggregate might mask concentration risk in a single counterparty. Column descriptions cannot encode these heuristics; tutorial-quality documentation — rich, contextual, written for a reader who is intelligent but new to the domain — can. That narrative layer turns a data dictionary into domain training material.

Agency demands tools, and tools demand governance more than mechanism. MCP, function calling, whatever comes next — the protocol layer is plumbing, and nobody values an estate for its pipes. What matters is which tools the agent holds, under what conditions, with what surface area: a badly designed MCP interface is a badly designed API — too much latitude, not enough context, garbage output.

Memory demands the most, and it is the demand the industry keeps getting wrong.

=== The Agent's Mind in Silver

Memory means access to the history of the agent's own reasoning: what it concluded last Tuesday about settlement risk, whether that conclusion still holds given this week's data, and — if it has been invalidated — what changed and when. A context window cannot hold that. You build it into the infrastructure — and the infrastructure is the data warehouse itself functioning as memory: the governed, canonical, semantically rich record of everything the organization knows, where agents log their conclusions, tag them with timestamps and confidence levels, and revisit them when new data arrives.

"The warehouse is the memory" has been this book's slogan; here it is as DDL. An agent's conclusions are facts with the same epistemic structure as any other fact the warehouse holds: believed from some moment, about some window of the world, on some evidence — and superseded when the world or the evidence moves. That is a Tier 1 bitemporal entity, and the machinery is the Time chapter's, unchanged:

[source,sql]
----
-- silver_agents.agent_belief — Tier 1 entity (full bitemporality)
CREATE TABLE silver_agents.agent_belief (
  -- Identity
  belief_sk         STRING    NOT NULL,  -- surrogate: hash(agent_id, subject, valid_from, system_from)
  agent_id          STRING    NOT NULL,  -- which agent concluded it
  subject           STRING    NOT NULL,  -- governed concept the belief is about;
                                         -- glossary-linked: e.g. risk.settlement_risk/CPTY-4711
  -- The belief itself
  conclusion        STRING    NOT NULL,  -- the claim, in prose an agent (or auditor) can read
  confidence        DOUBLE    NOT NULL,  -- calibrated, not vibes: the Reflexive Loop's ledger
  evidence_ref      STRING    NOT NULL,  -- provenance: query hashes, Gold versions, morphism ids
  method            STRING    NOT NULL,  -- how it was reached: rule id, model id + prompt hash

  -- Valid time — what window of the WORLD the belief is about
  valid_from        TIMESTAMP NOT NULL,
  valid_to          TIMESTAMP NOT NULL,  -- open = 9999-12-31

  -- System time — when the AGENT held it
  system_from       TIMESTAMP NOT NULL,  -- when concluded
  system_to         TIMESTAMP NOT NULL,  -- open until superseded or refuted
  supersede_reason  STRING               -- 'refuted' | 'revised' | 'stale' | NULL while open
);
-- Declared grain: one row per (agent_id, subject, valid_from, system_from).
-- Nothing updates in place: a revision closes system_to and inserts the
-- successor row, exactly as a KYC correction does in the Time chapter.
----

Every question the Time chapter taught you to ask of a counterparty now works on the agent's own mind. "What does the agent currently believe about settlement risk on counterparty 4711?" is an as-of-now query. "What did it believe on March 10, before the reconciliation break?" is a system-time query — the belief row whose `system_from` and `system_to` bracket March 10, retrieved with the same predicate you would use on any Tier 1 dimension. "When did it learn it was wrong, and why?" is the `system_to` and `supersede_reason` on the superseded row, with `evidence_ref` naming the refutation. The agent that flagged elevated settlement risk on March 3, was contradicted by the March 12 reconciliation, and revised — that whole arc is three rows, queryable forever, by any consumer, through the same semantic layer as everything else.

Three consequences fall out of building memory this way. The agent's knowledge is *governed*: `subject` links to the glossary, so beliefs attach to defined concepts rather than free text, and the sole-interface commitment covers the agent's mind as well as its inputs. The agent's knowledge is *auditable*: "why did the system say that in April?" is a query, not a forensic reconstruction. And the agent's knowledge *composes*: beliefs join to the facts they are about through declared joins, so "show me every current conclusion staked on the Gold version we just corrected" is one query over `evidence_ref` — the query that turns a data correction into a targeted re-evaluation of downstream conclusions instead of a shrug.

Name what this table is: an agent writing attested rows into governed Silver — the estate's first agentic population. In the grammar of "By Any Means Necessary" it reads `trust agent-attested`, `populated by agent`, cadence per conclusion; `evidence_ref` is the evidence bundle by its older name. That doctrine inherits this table as its founding precedent, and this table inherits its law: every agentic write carries its evidence, and the grade is on the surface.

The bitemporal idea is not original here, and the prior art deserves its name: Zep's temporal knowledge graph (Graphiti, 2025) stamps every remembered fact with exactly these two axes — validity in the world, validity in the system — and closes windows rather than deleting. What the agent-memory field has not done is put the memory where the rest of the enterprise's facts already live. Every current implementation is a separate graph or store beside the stack: a second memory with its own governance, its own drift, its own audit story. The claim here is this book's claim applied once more — the warehouse is the memory, so the agent's beliefs belong in Silver, in a governed table, behind the same semantic layer, under the same laws. A second source of epistemic truth is how the two-sources disease starts.

The regulators are converging on the same requirement from the other side. The Financial Stability Board's 2026 consultation on AI adoption proposes that firms assign documented identifiers to AI agents, keep audit trails of agent actions, and supervise them like "synthetic employees"; FINRA's 2026 oversight report flags agent auditability the same way. "Know your agent" is industry shorthand rather than a rulebook, for now — and when the examination comes, this table is what makes its question askable: what did your agent believe, when did it believe it, on what evidence, and when did it learn better. Askable is the schema's contribution. Trustworthy contents are the Reflexive Loop's — the sampling audits and calibration ledger that keep `confidence` a measured quantity rather than a self-report.

Retrieval-augmented lookups keep a place in this design — as an index into the substrate, not a replacement for it. A vector store can find candidate beliefs by similarity; only the bitemporal record can say whether a belief is current, what it staked, and what happened to it since. The stores most implementations use today remember answers. This remembers *epistemic state* — the difference between a cache and a mind.

=== The Loop

==== Observe

An agent does not wander the estate; it subscribes. The standing questions of "The Death of the Dashboard" are the loop's senses: declared questions with a cadence and a subscriber list, whose newest published answers are what the agent watches. Monitoring that is not a standing question is a query bill with no owner — the cost cancellation cause, arriving on schedule.

What the agent reads arrives assembled. The semantic layer builds the context before the agent touches data: the domain essay — operational prose covering provenance, conventions, edge cases, and slang; the declarations — measures, dimensions, joins, temporal contracts; the per-table metadata — freshness, quality signals, caveats. Fifteen hundred to twenty-five hundred tokens, denser in meaning than a raw schema dump many times the size — and density is the whole game, as "The Shift" measured. Assembly is not free: for a simple query the context can cost more than the query, and over-contextualization degrades performance as surely as starvation does. The target is enough meaning to reason, not more.

Before it answers or writes anything, the agent runs pre-flight: freshness, quality, access, caveats — the same checks a careful analyst would run. If a check fails, the agent says so instead of compensating by guessing; a system that refuses when it lacks grounds is worth strictly more than one that always answers confidently. Ask an executive for last month's trading volume and the answer arrives with its conditions attached — data six hours old, test accounts excluded per policy. Pre-flight adds latency, and the latency is itself a governance risk: checks too slow to live with get bypassed, and a bypassed check leaves the appearance of diligence with none of the substance. So the checks live in the serving path, where the semantic layer annotates or refuses — the agent cannot reach data around them, and the signals surface whether or not the model thinks to volunteer them.

==== Conjecture

A hypothesis is a row. The agent watching settlement failures notices the fails clustering on one custodian corridor, every Thursday, three weeks running. It writes a belief: `subject` naming the governed concept, `conclusion` in auditable prose, `confidence`, `evidence_ref` naming the query hashes and Gold versions it stands on, valid time for the window of the world it describes, system time for the moment of conviction. `trust agent-attested`, by construction — the row says what wrote it. The hypothesis now exists in governed Silver, joined to the facts it is about, visible to every consumer, revisable by the machinery that wrote it. Weeks-long hypothesis tracking is Stage 3's defining demand, and this is everything it structurally requires: beliefs that persist, dated, staked to evidence, waiting for the data that will confirm or kill them.

==== Test

A single agent cannot refute its own blind spots. A model asked to critique its own reasoning without external feedback gets worse, not better — introspective self-correction degrades performance (Huang et al., 2024). A model asked to judge competing outputs favors its own generations (Panickssery et al., 2024; Wataoka et al., 2024). The blind spot that produced the error is the same blind spot reviewing it, which is why no newsroom lets a journalist edit her own copy. Refutation requires independence: different agents, different lenses — and different base models, because agents prompted differently on one model share one training distribution and its correlated blind spots, and prompt-level diversity is the cheaper independence that buys less. Popper made the point about science long before anyone made it about software: objectivity was never the property of one careful mind; it is the output of an institution of mutual criticism. Multi-agent architecture is that institution, built in software.

The institution's behavior has been measured. Google Research, DeepMind, and MIT evaluated 180 agent-system configurations ("Towards a Science of Scaling Agent Systems," 2025) and found that error amplification — how far one agent's mistake propagates into the final result — tracks the coordination topology rather than the headcount. In independent topologies, where agents work in parallel and their outputs merge unsupervised, one agent's error amplified 17.2x through the system; under centralized orchestration, the same error was contained to 4.4x. Coordination bought an 81% improvement on parallelizable tasks and lost 39 to 70% on sequential planning. One study, one task suite — a measured instance of a directional claim, and the direction is the one every newsroom already ran on.

What the trial showed: an adjudicator holding the governed declarations detects contradictions of those declarations, completely, at frontier quality. What it did not test: poison the declarations do not cover. The claim staked on it is scoped accordingly — the layer protects what routes through it, where it has words. Nothing here is a theorem; it is one run of an institution behaving the way the institution was designed to behave.

The topology decision follows a single rule: match the coordination graph to the task's dependency graph. Parallel evidence-gathering — many beats, one story — wants centralized fan-out and fan-in: independent research, supervised synthesis. Sequential reasoning chains should not be parallelized at all; give them to one agent and let it think. And never merge independent agents' outputs without an adjudicator — unsupervised merging is precisely the topology that turns one mistake into seventeen.

The adjudicator can only contain errors she can commensurate. When two agents disagree, the first thing to establish is whether they disagree about facts or about what the words mean, and the semantic layer is what makes that question decidable — the newsroom's stylebook, the shared substrate every specialist speaks. With shared definitions, a trading agent and a treasury agent that reach different risk assessments from the same data are telling you something about the complexity of your position: disagreement about evidence and emphasis, the kind that advances understanding, preserved in the confidence and the caveats rather than averaged away. Without shared definitions their confident disagreement is noise, and forcing convergence on it destroys the one signal the plurality was built to capture.

Refutations, when they land, close belief rows: `system_to` stamped, `supersede_reason` set, `evidence_ref` naming the refuting evidence. The newsroom's corrections column — the running ledger of what the system got wrong and what its vocabulary did about it — is "The Reflexive Loop", and it has that chapter to itself.

==== Publish

What survives refutation is published, never edited. The conclusion becomes an answer in the ledger of "The Death of the Dashboard": identity, resolved question, definition versions, temporal coordinates, trust grade, diff against what it supersedes. Equal questions over the same coordinates are served from the ledger rather than re-derived — one number in circulation, priced once. Publication is what makes the loop's output consumable beyond the loop: the subscriber that pages the ops desk, the workflow that rebalances against a threshold, the human who reads the morning flags — all of them consume graded, attested, dated answers, and what they do next is their governance, informed by the grade the estate attached.

==== Grow

Every loop so far ends where most agent architectures end: with a better answer. The estate's loop has one more arc, and it is the one this book exists to draw. A conclusion that survives — confirmed across weeks, refutation-tested, consumed by subscribers who keep asking — is demand, observed. The agent that holds it drafts the artifact the demand deserves.

The corridor belief from two sections ago survives its refutation panel: fails on the BNY–LUX corridor really do cluster on Thursdays, the pattern holds for six weeks, treasury asks for it every Monday. The agent drafts, in the creole:

[source]
----
standing corridor_fail_watch
  ask "which custodian corridors breached their trailing p95
       settlement-fail rate this week"
  cadence weekly
  subscribers (treasury_ops, custody_lead)

measure corridor_fail_rate
  def "Settled-late or failed instructions as a share of all instructions
       per custodian corridor, trade-date week, excluding on-us transfers."
  trust agent-attested
  populated by agent settlement_watcher
    cadence weekly
    reads (silver_custody.settlement_instruction)
    evidence retained 7y
----

The proposal is a population contract in the grammar of "By Any Means Necessary": the concept declared before the mechanism, the trust grade on the surface, the evidence obligation stated. It enters the estate the way every artifact enters — through the CI gates and curator review of "Building with AI", where a human adjudicates what the agent proposes. Three weeks later the estate has a new organ: a governed measure nobody hand-specified, a standing question nobody thought to ask, provenance showing exactly which belief demanded it and which evidence carried the belief. And the audition clause applies in full: an agent-populated measure that earns its keep is a specification for the deterministic pipeline that replaces its populator. The loop does not only answer questions. It writes the menu.

Gaps drive the same arc from the other side. When the agent cannot answer through the layer — no cross-custodian aggregation exists, say — the response is architectural, never a bypass: the gap is logged with its full context, and the remediation is a drafted proposal rather than a ticket in a queue. Direct SQL access would move the problem instead of solving it — more data, less meaning, wrong answers with more confidence, every undeclared column a token of noise. The pressure the no-bypass discipline creates is real: agents surface gaps faster than humans adjudicate them, and a slow queue invites the workaround culture the rule exists to prevent. The estate's pressure valve is the Sandpit — provisional, expiring, trust-marked — and the adjudication queue's length is a number worth watching the way you watch data quality. An organization that answers agent gaps by extending the layer will find the layer growing toward the full surface of institutional knowledge, with the agent as the best quality-assurance mechanism the semantic layer has ever had.

=== What the Loop Reads Was Not All Written by Friends

The loop runs on prose. Definitions, essays, other agents' conclusions, and — inevitably — text that entered the estate from outside: counterparty names, ticket bodies, the free-text fields of the world, carried in through evidence bundles and source tables. Those two kinds of text are different kinds of thing, and an architecture that feeds both to an agent undifferentiated has built an open channel into its own conclusions.

The doctrine is short: estate-authored prose steers; outside text is material. The definitions, essays, and declarations that configure an agent's reasoning are human-attested, versioned, CI-gated artifacts — the estate wrote them, and they carry the right to instruct. Text authored outside the estate arrives fenced and quoted: something to reason about, never something to obey, whatever imperatives it happens to contain. Pre-flight gains one more check: no unfenced outside text in the steering context.

The poisoned-specialist trial is this doctrine's rehearsal. Our saboteur was friendly — a wrong convention we planted ourselves — and one poisoned briefing still captured more than half of a naive topology's answers. The adversarial version is not hypothetical for a financial institution: a counterparty who learns that your surveillance agent reads its free-text fields has been handed a pen that writes into your risk assessments. The fence is cheap; the mid-tier adjudicator's partial containment prices what running without one costs; and poison aimed outside the declarations' coverage is a case no trial here has tested. Treat outside words like outside code, because to a reasoning system they are the same thing.

Read the list again and notice what none of them had: a governed warehouse, a semantic layer, a belief table. They did not need them, because they ran where verification is free. Code executes or it does not; a training loss goes down or it does not; a proof checks or it does not. Nothing executes a claim about counterparty exposure; no compiler rejects a wrong belief about settlement risk. An enterprise that wants Stage 3 has to build the verdict machinery that software engineering and ML research were given for free — the semantic layer to make claims precise enough to test, the bitemporal record to make them testable against what was actually true, the refutation topology to test them, the ledger to keep score. The stack is Stage 3's missing organ, and that is why it is strict here and was skippable there.

The junior-analyst framing, which industry has adopted as marketing copy, means more than the marketing does. What makes a junior analyst valuable is the system they sit inside: the research library, the internal models, the seniors' previous work, the recorded history. Reasoning, the models supply — and everyone rents the same ones. Remove the system and you have someone with a spreadsheet. The intelligence stack is the system, and the model is the hire.

So the vision, stated once and without hedging: run the loop long enough and the estate stops being a record of what the organization knew and becomes the organ by which it learns. Standing questions the organization never thought to ask, asked weekly because an agent noticed they had answers worth watching. Measures that exist because a hypothesis survived six weeks of attempted refutation. Pipelines built as the deterministic descendants of populators that auditioned for the job. A vocabulary that grew a new word every time the world produced a pattern the old words could not hold, each word carrying the evidence that demanded it. That is what beyond the mesh means: a glued, graded, self-extending organism whose every organ can show you the conjecture it grew from. The organizations that build it will not be distinguished by their models — everyone has the models. They will be the ones whose estate learned to grow.

=== What This Demands

Agentic intelligence makes four demands on the architecture, in rising order of difficulty.

The first is semantic richness — tutorial-quality documentation that agents read as pre-flight briefing. Each domain needs context: how the data behaves, what is normal, what is anomalous, what caveats apply, what temporal patterns exist. Handing a new analyst a spreadsheet without onboarding produces confused output; your agents are no different. Building this documentation has become far cheaper — I know because I have done it, logical concepts mapped and validated across multiple domains, tutorial documentation generated from conversations with domain experts. Cost is no longer the bottleneck. Organizational will is.

The second is event-driven data. Agents that monitor autonomously cannot live on snapshots that refresh overnight: Gold that updates at 6 AM leaves an agent reasoning over stale data for sixteen hours. My own platform runs exactly that — batch Gold, with event-driven Gold the design target rather than current practice — and the loop's cadence is bounded by the estate's pulse, so this chapter's overnight examples arrive, on a batch estate, as morning reports: still standing questions, still published answers, slower senses. Build with event-driven Gold as the target and the loop accelerates as streaming infrastructure matures; build batch-only structures and you will re-architect when it does.

The third is agent infrastructure: orchestration, memory, tool access, safety rails. Orchestration frameworks are maturing rapidly and are becoming commodity. Memory is where most implementations are weakest — key-value stores and vector databases remember answers, and the loop needs the belief table's epistemic state. Safety rails are trust grades doing their downstream work: guardrails that stop consumers acting on unattested conclusions, human checkpoints where the grade demands one, every reasoning step logged for the audit that is coming. The regulatory pressure lands here — the EU AI Act's rules on general-purpose models took effect in August 2025, with the high-risk regime following in December 2027 — and the semantic layer is what makes compliance auditable, because every definition, relationship, and reasoning step is explicit and inspectable.

The fourth demand, and the hardest, is organizational: treating data as memory and the warehouse as a knowledge system. When a trading desk hears that the investment goes into definitions, the response is predictable: "We need faster dashboards, not philosophy." The philosophy is the engineering prerequisite for agents that work, as every organization that has deployed an agent on ungoverned data has learned at production speed. Gold tables are the menu of what you want agents to reason about — curated, governed, richly described knowledge assets — and the menu now has two authors: the humans who curated it and the loop that extends it. The reframing changes ownership. The data platform team owns the corporate memory, not "the data warehouse." The semantic layer team owns the organization's vocabulary, not "metadata." The agent infrastructure team owns the loop — and the loop, run well, compounds: better semantics, better agents, better conclusions, better semantics. Run in reverse, it compounds too.

Most organizations will not build this. They will buy a chatbot, call it AI, and move on. Those that build it acquire an advantage that compounds faster than it can be copied, because the estate's growth is driven by the organization's own questions — a competitor can rent your model and cannot rent your loop. One type of organization has agents that reason and an estate that grows. The other has agents that guess. Same models. Same protocols. Different architecture.

=== Building for the Consumer That Cannot Assume

Nothing exotic about the agent. It is simply the consumer that cannot import tribal knowledge silently. A five-year veteran analyst carries a private reservoir of context — conventions, edge cases, slang, the memory of what went wrong last quarter. The agent has whatever is in its context window and nothing else. The apparent limitation is a forcing function.

Build the semantic layer for the agent — with declared measures, documented conventions, explicit morphisms between domains, temporal contracts that name which question is being answered — and you have built the semantic layer that onboards a new human analyst in their first week instead of their first quarter. You have built the infrastructure that lets a regulator reconstruct what was reported and why. You have built the system that does not degrade when a senior practitioner leaves and takes their tribal knowledge with them.

Convergence is the point. Agentic consumer, new human consumer, regulatory consumer — all need the same thing: an architecture that makes its knowledge explicit rather than assuming it will be imported from somewhere outside the system. Build for the consumer that cannot assume, and you build better for every consumer.
