== The Living Colimit

Everything to this point is architecture you can build with today's tools and today's discipline. Assemble those parts into one system, let it run, and what they become is the most ambitious claim in this book. The colimit is a verb. Run it continuously and it stops being a diagram you draw and becomes an organism you operate: a semantic model of the whole enterprise that verifies itself, repairs itself locally, reports its own health, stays correct as the business moves under it — and, in the next chapter, doubts itself, productively. A *living* colimit. The organisation that has one has agents that reason over its entire institutional memory and can be trusted to. The organisation that does not has agents that guess. That gap is a category difference, not a feature difference — and it is open to be built now, with the tools already on the shelf.

=== What the Living Colimit Is

Three things you already have, wired into one loop.

The **domains and bridges** are the body: each domain its own catalogued ontology, each bridge an explicit, dated span recording what maps, what is lost, what is partial. The **verification mesh** — Continuous Morphism Verification, the pattern in "Building with AI" — is the nervous system: a standing set of per-domain agents that, on every scan and on every change, re-check that each bridge still holds against live data, and a coordinator that runs the cross-domain commutativity checks no single agent can. And **colimit-health** is the vital sign: a live, rolled-up measure of how connected, honest, fresh, and consistent the whole glued structure is, watched the way you watch uptime, alerting when it degrades.

Run those together and the system has a property no hand-maintained model ever had: when meaning breaks, it says so, *locally, with a witness.* A regulator reclassifies an entity overnight; the domain agent catches the schema change on its scan; the coordinator re-runs the commutativity check for the affected concept; one routing now disagrees with another; a witness is raised on that single span and routed to the one human who owns it. By morning the health view shows one amber span with a named cause — not a green dashboard hiding a silent break, and not a surprise found at an audit a season later. Meaning has become a monitored, measured, self-defending quantity. That is the living colimit.

=== The Architecture, As a Thing You Build

The mesh is real infrastructure. A standing multi-agent verification system is itself a large, failure-prone thing that needs its own operations — so treat it as exactly that: load-bearing infrastructure with its own observability, not magic. Designed, not waved at:

* **Per-domain verification agents.** Each owns the bridges incident to its domain and runs the five within-reach checks every scan and on every schema/ontology change (CI-for-meaning, triggered like CI-for-code): mapped fields still exist, keys still join at the documented cardinality, distributions have not shifted in a way that implies a changed definition, no unaccounted-for new attribute, documented losses still the actual losses.
* **The coordinator.** Runs the sixth, inherently cross-domain check — commutativity, the colimit's universal property used as the test — assembles the witness when it fails, maintains the global health vector, and routes exceptions to owners. It is the most load-bearing component, so it is monitored like any other critical service: its own latency, queue depth, scan coverage, and false-positive rate are dashboards, and it has an on-call.
* **Witness-driven repair.** A failure does not emit "the model is broken." It emits *the concept and the two paths that disagree* — the smallest possible repair surface. Trivial drift auto-repairs; genuine judgment routes to one human. Humans do adjudication; the mesh does verification. The inverse of the old world, where humans rubber-stamped attestations and a steward caste did the translation.
* **Colimit-health as a live eval.** Not a deliverable that reaches 100% and ships, but a metric you operate against forever: morphism completeness, bridge honesty, concept coverage, staleness, commutativity. You ship at 80% health on the seven domains that matter this quarter and raise it continuously, the way a model team operates against an eval suite rather than waiting for a perfect score.

=== Shipping It Within a Year

The reason this is buildable now and not a someday-vision is that it composes from parts already in production, and it delivers value at every intermediate step — finishability, made operational.

* *First — a few domains, the bridges between them, nightly verification.* Stand up the colimit over the five-to-seven domains that carry the questions you ask. Write the bridges. Run the mesh nightly. You now have a cross-domain semantic surface and a health number. This alone is ahead of almost every organisation alive.
* *Second — continuous and on-change triggers, and the explore.* Move verification from nightly to on-commit (CI-for-meaning in earnest), and expose the glued ontology as the live cross-domain explore of "Exploring the Glued Ontology." Now the thing is queryable *and* self-checking.
* *Third — the autonomous curation loop.* The mesh stops only flagging and starts proposing: drafting the morphism that closes a detected gap, the provisional semantics for a new Dev Gold feed, the repair for a drifted bridge — humans adjudicating, agents doing the work. The colimit now grows and heals largely on its own.

=== Scale: A Build Plan

Commutativity checking is combinatorial in the number of paths, and the sweet spot is five to thirty domains — which is most organisations, and exactly where the value is. Beyond thirty densely-connected domains it becomes a build decision with two real options. **Hierarchical composition**: cluster tightly-related domains into sub-colimits, treat each as a single object, and verify between clusters at a higher level — colimits of colimits, bounding path length, the same construction recursively. Or **accept and declare** that distant domain pairs are never directly verified, and say so on the health view rather than implying coverage you do not have. Both are engineering; neither is a fudge. The 5–30 regime ships first; the hierarchical regime is the following year's work, and it is tractable because the unit of composition does not change — only the level you apply it at.

=== The Formal Spine

The construction has proofs, and none of the mathematics is new — it is the specification-gluing calculus from my earlier life, transported to ontologies an LLM can read. What follows is the precise form of the three results the living colimit relies on, with enough of each proof that a mathematically literate practitioner can check the reasoning rather than take it on faith.

First, the objects, precisely. A domain is a finite-limit sketch: a finite graph of entities and operations, plus a finite set of imposed cones that carry the rules — partiality, multiplicity, identification. A bridge is a *span*: two structure-preserving maps out of a shared sub-theory, one into each domain. (The Architecture chapter's "bridge functor" is the working shorthand; the span is the object the theorems govern, and the distinction matters — a functor from Trading into Finance would make one domain part of the other, and the whole point of a bridge is that neither side owns the other.) Integration is the pushout of two domains along their shared sub-theory, and the colimit of the whole bridge diagram for many — taken over the *presentations*, the syntactic artifacts the pipeline actually stores, never by gluing the categories themselves, where pushouts misbehave. The sharing rule from "Multi-Domain Composition" is not commentary on this construction; it is its standing hypothesis. A span is *clean* when the shared sub-theory embeds faithfully in both domains with its rules intact — which is exactly what the sharing rule enforces: share only what the domains genuinely agree on, split and document everything else.

**Consistency is commutativity.** Say the integration is consistent when no domain's distinct concepts get silently merged in the composed view — joint faithfulness, in the jargon; "the colimit never confuses two things a domain keeps separate," in practice. For a finite diagram of clean spans, consistency holds exactly when the diagram commutes: every two bridge-paths that carry the same shared concept between the same two domains agree. The proof is short enough to sketch. The colimit is the disjoint union of every domain's concepts, quotiented by the bridge identifications; a spurious merge inside one domain can only arise from a chain of identifications that leaves the domain through one shared concept and returns through another — which is precisely a pair of bridge-paths disagreeing. Commuting diagram, no such pair, no merge; non-commuting diagram, and the disagreeing pair *is* the merge. Because the diagram is finite, the failure is computable and specific: one concept, two paths — the witness the mesh raises.

The witness is worth seeing once in the flesh. Take the concept "the legal entity by which Compliance knows this counterparty." The direct Trading-to-Compliance bridge carries it as the counterparty's own legal entity. The indirect route — Trading to Finance to Compliance — carries it as the holder of the booking account. For most counterparties the two routes agree. For a prime-brokerage master account that books trades for two underlying legal entities, they do not: the naive colimit would quietly identify counterparties that merely co-book. The commutativity check fails on exactly that concept and that pair of paths, and the repair is the sharing rule doing its job: the two routes were never carrying one concept — split legal-entity identity from booking-account-holder identity, record the consolidation as documented partiality on the Finance leg, and the diagram commutes again.

**Gluing schemas glues instances.** For a clean span, an instance of the glued schema is exactly a pair of domain instances that agree on the shared sub-theory — and for a whole commuting diagram, a tuple of domain instances agreeing on every shared part. Composition of definitions done right *is* composition of data done right: not a union of rows but the canonical join of compatible instances along shared identity. The structural reason fits in a sentence — turning theories into their instance categories flips colimits into limits, the way it always does — and the exactness of the agreement, on the nose rather than up to coherence bookkeeping, is bought by cleanliness. The strictness of the theorem lives in a governance rule a practitioner can enforce, not in a technical assumption nobody can check.

**Finishability — conditionally, and the condition is the point.** Any sub-assembly of the diagram embeds faithfully in the whole, *provided the enlarged diagram still commutes*. Ship the seven-domain colimit this quarter; glue on the eighth next quarter; nothing already built is invalidated — unless the new bridges break commutativity, in which case the check fails loudly, with a witness, before the damage propagates. That conditional is not a weakness to hide. It is the reason the mesh is continuous rather than a one-time gate: every addition re-obligates the check, and the theorem is precisely the statement that passing it is sufficient.

The honest boundaries, so nobody overclaims on my behalf: the results hold for finite diagrams of clean spans with set-valued instances; "consistency" here means no spurious identification, not data quality and not logical satisfiability; the commutativity check is decidable but worst-case exponential in path enumeration, which is why the construction's sweet spot is five to thirty domains with hierarchical composition beyond; and the mathematics guarantees soundness *given* that a bridge's declared losses are accurate — verifying the declarations against live data is not a theorem, it is the mesh's job, and the previous chapters built it. One more consequence falls out of the universal property for free: for a given diagram there is exactly one canonical cross-domain view, so any hand-built cross-domain mart either agrees with the colimit or is wrong relative to the bridges it claims to respect. The math was settled decades ago. This chapter says the thing it licenses is buildable; the Playbook says how to start.

=== Why It Is Worth Building

Reframe what you would own. Not "a data warehouse" — the organisation's memory, verified. Not "metadata" — its vocabulary, kept current by machines. Not "a chatbot" — a reasoning layer that can be trusted across the whole business because the meaning underneath it is continuously checked rather than periodically attested. That is a category of system the industry has named in pieces and built in none. The parts are commodity now: the models, the protocols, the medallion substrate, the semantic layer. What is scarce is the assembly and the will. Build it, or watch someone else build it first. The Playbook is how you start, Monday.
